## hexagram 7: Multitude

**Multitude (師 / shī), also called The Army** ䷆  
*organization, perseverance, shared strength, care for the people*

```text
Top       ━━  ━━  6
          ━━  ━━  5
          ━━  ━━  4
          ━━  ━━  3
          ━━━━━━  2
Bottom    ━━  ━━  1
```

Earth above, water below. The image in the source text is groundwater held within the earth: a resource you cannot see from the surface, gathered and protected rather than spent all at once. Hexagram 7 is concerned with a multitude and what it takes to bring many energies into a coherent effort. Its traditional military language is severe; the question underneath it is practical. How do you organize strength without turning everyone involved into a resource to be consumed?

The Judgment speaks of perseverance and capable leadership. Its commentary makes a distinction worth keeping: discipline is necessary, but it should not be achieved by force. Trust matters. A person coordinating an effort needs responsibility and the confidence of the people involved, not a blank check to override them. The text also treats war as dangerous and destructive, a last resort rather than a model for every ordinary disagreement.

You don't need to imagine yourself commanding an army for this to apply. The multitude might be a household trying to stay afloat, a team with too many moving parts, or the competing obligations on your own calendar. Give the work a clear purpose. Decide who is doing what, what resources are available, and what would make you stop and reassess. A plan can be firm without being cruel.

The Image adds something the language of command can obscure: strength grows through generosity toward people. If you want an effort to last, the people carrying it need food, rest, a say in what affects them, and enough stability to keep showing up. This includes you. A schedule that only works when nobody gets sick, tired, or overwhelmed is not a reliable structure. It's a wish with a spreadsheet attached.

### the cast: no changing lines

All six lines in this cast are stable. There are **no changing lines** and no relating hexagram in the saved reading. That doesn't mean your circumstances can never change; it means this cast gives us no moving-line instruction or second hexagram to interpret. Stay with Multitude's Judgment and Image rather than inventing a transition.

The solid second line sits among five yielding lines, with water beneath earth. As an image, it can suggest a steady center inside a larger field of needs and responsibilities. You can ask what holds the effort together without assuming that you personally have to be the center of everything. Sometimes the steady center is a shared agreement, a working routine, or a person who knows when to hand off the job.

*legacy, family, security*

The Ten of Pentacles brings in the structures you inherited or helped build: the family system, the material foundation, and the rules about what counts as a secure life. There may be real support here, even if it came with expectations you no longer want to carry. Name both. A tradition can have given you something useful without having the final word on how you live.

Read alongside Multitude, the past card asks who was included in the promise of security and who was expected to maintain it. If one person did all the invisible work, that is part of the inheritance too. You can keep the care and change the distribution of labor. Gratitude doesn't require reenacting every arrangement that came before you.

The Eight of Pentacles puts the emphasis on practice. The card isn't a demand to become endlessly productive. It is an invitation to build competence through repeated, deliberate work.

Multitude gives that practice a container. One person can learn a skill, but an ongoing effort also needs priorities, resources, and a reasonable division of responsibility. Decide what today's work is for before you commit to doing more of it. If the answer is simply that everyone else expects you to be available, that deserves another look.

## the synthesis

The tarot shows a movement from inherited security, through present practice, toward a values-based choice. Hexagram 7 doesn't contradict that sequence. It asks what kind of organization makes the choice sustainable. A good intention needs a structure that can hold it, and a structure is only as humane as the way it treats the people inside it.

This morning's **The Fool (reversed)** adds the question of risk: are you rushing past the details, or are you waiting for a certainty no one can provide? Neither extreme has to run the day. The Eight of Pentacles offers a testable next step; Multitude asks you to put that step in order and consider its effect on everyone involved. The Lovers asks whether the eventual commitment is one you actually want.

Try this with one concrete obligation. Write down the purpose, the next action, the person responsible, and the support needed to make it possible. If you are that person, include your own limits in the plan. If someone else is involved, ask rather than assume what they can take on. Then leave space to revise the arrangement when you have better information.

You don't have to turn every difficulty into a battle to take it seriously. You can protect what matters, distribute the work fairly, and choose a direction without demanding that everyone march at the same pace. That's a sturdier kind of coordination, and it leaves room for the people doing the living.
