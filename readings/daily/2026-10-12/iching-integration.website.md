**大有 · dàyǒu ䷍**
*heaven below, flame above, possession in great measure*

Fire in heaven. Wilhelm reads it as the sun at its height, shining over everything at once, so that all things stand out in the light and become visible. The lower trigram is heaven, the creative: strength. The upper trigram is flame, the clinging: clarity. Strength inside, clarity outside. That's the whole condition of Great Harvest, and the judgment doesn't hedge about it: "POSSESSION IN GREAT MEASURE. / Supreme success."

The strange part is who holds it. Five of the six lines are solid. The only broken line sits in the fifth place, the ruler's seat, and the strong lines arrange themselves around it. The commentary asks the obvious question (how does the weak line hold the strong ones?) and answers it: unselfish modesty. The abundance here isn't seized. It gathers around whoever doesn't grab.

The image adds the catch. Sunlight shows everything, the good and the bad alike, so a harvest this size has to be administered. "Thus the superior man curbs evil and furthers good." Having a lot is half of it. What you do once it's all visible is the other half.

### the changing lines

**Line 2: "A big wagon for loading. / One may undertake something. / No blame."** Wilhelm is specific: great possession isn't measured by quantity. It's measured by mobility and use. A full barn that can't move anything is just storage. The big wagon means capable helpers, people who can carry real weight, and the line says you can load responsibility onto them. Undertake something. It's allowed now.

**Line 5: "He whose truth is accessible, yet dignified, / Has good fortune."** This is the ruling line, the yielding one everything else orbits. People come to you through sincerity, not pressure. But the commentary adds a warning: kindness alone isn't enough when there's this much on the table, because insolence starts to spread. Openness needs a spine. Be reachable and keep your boundaries in the same breath.

Both lines change, and the cast moves into **hexagram 13, Seeking Harmony** (同人, fellowship with men in the open). The trigrams flip: flame below, heaven above. Possession turns into community. The judgment there says "It furthers one to cross the great water," and the image is about organizing, about making distinctions between things so that a group becomes an order instead of a crowd.

## the spread

**The Fool** (past)
*beginnings, innocence, spontaneity*

**The Hanged Man** (present)
*surrender, pause, perspective*

**King of Pentacles** (future)
*abundance, stewardship, discipline*

All three upright, all three clean. The Fool in the past is the leap you already took, probably without much of a plan, which is the only way the Fool ever leaps. The Hanged Man in the present is the suspension that followed: hanging still, seeing everything upside down, giving up the urge to force the next step. Then the King of Pentacles, the most grounded figure in the deck. Not someone who got lucky. Someone who built something and keeps it running.

## the thread

This morning's **Six of Cups** set the undertone: nostalgia, kindness, innocence, the old image of one child handing another a cup of flowers. The cards today (the Fool, the Hanged Man, and the King of Pentacles) were the surface. Hexagram 14 is what's underneath.

And look, the overlap is almost too neat. The King of Pentacles is Great Harvest wearing a crown, abundance and stewardship in one figure. The I Ching doesn't contradict the tarot. It contextualizes it, and the context is the part the King card alone doesn't say: how you hold the harvest matters as much as whether it arrives.

The Hanged Man is where that gets interesting. Surrender, in the middle position, reads like loss when you're inside it. Hexagram 14 reframes it. The weak line in the ruler's seat holds the strong lines through modesty, not force. The Hanged Man's yielding isn't the thing standing between you and abundance. It's the posture that lets abundance gather. You can't grip a harvest this size, only arrange yourself so it has somewhere to land.

The Six of Cups and line 5 belong together too. The morning card is kindness without calculation, the cup handed over freely. Line 5 says that's exactly how people get won here, through "unaffected sincerity," but it also says sincerity needs dignity next to it. Generous, yes. A pushover, no.

Then line 2, the big wagon. The Fool started alone, as the Fool always does. Hexagram 13 is where this ends up: fellowship in the open, a group organized around something larger than any one person's interest. The harvest isn't meant to stay in one set of hands. Line 3 isn't moving today, but it says the same thing from another angle: a petty person keeps great possession for themselves and gets harmed by it, while a generous one offers it outward. The wagon means you load it up and you let other people help carry it.

So the arc runs like this. The Fool leaps. The Hanged Man stops and sees differently. The King keeps what grows. Underneath all three, the hexagram asks a practical question: what's the harvest for, and who else is it supposed to reach? Name one thing you could actually put on the wagon today, and one person capable enough to help pull it.
